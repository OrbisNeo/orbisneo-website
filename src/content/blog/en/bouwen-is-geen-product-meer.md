In early October, a single developer put seven free counterparts to Adobe programs online: Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Lightroom, After Effects, InDesign and Acrobat. Not sketches or demos, but programs you can download and open. [Ars Technica described](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/10/software-is-over-bold-ai-developer-takes-aim-at-adobe-with-open-source-clones/) how he used an AI model to imitate the look, behaviour and features of Adobe's software, without copying Adobe's own code. [PetaPixel showed](https://petapixel.com/2026/10/07/someone-rebuilt-free-open-source-versions-of-photoshop-premiere-and-lightroom-using-ai/) how closely the copies resemble the originals. According to [How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/a-developer-used-ai-to-build-7-free-adobe-alternatives-and-subscription-software-has-a-new-problem/), the first recognisable versions were there within a few days.

It did not stop at Adobe. Within a week, counterparts to Word, Excel, PowerPoint, AutoCAD and Pro Tools followed. [Licenseware listed them](https://licenseware.io/ai-rebuilt-adobe-microsoft-apps-open-source/): a small team used AI to create credible first versions of about ten flagship applications in roughly a week.

And what works for design software works just as well for games. There too, everything a player sees and does reveals how the game is put together.

To be fair: these are early versions. [Creative Bloq tested them](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/i-tried-those-free-ai-coded-adobe-creative-cloud-clones-and-they-have-some-big-problems) and found considerable gaps, from missing camera and lens profiles to files that would not open straight away. But anyone who dismisses the news for that reason is looking at the wrong side of the story.

## Functionality is not property

Copyright protects code as it is written, not what that code does. In 2012 the European Court of Justice ruled that the functionality of a program, the programming language and the file formats are not protected, and that a user may observe, study and test a program to understand how it works. Licenseware summarises that case law clearly in the same piece.

Other forms of protection exist, such as patents, trademarks and licence terms. But they are narrower than many executives think, and slower to deploy.

In practice, the real protection of software was always something else: rebuilding was so expensive and time-consuming that nobody started. Anyone who wanted to copy Photoshop needed a large team and many years. The effort itself was the moat.

That moat is gone. The working program has become the blueprint. Every screen, every button, every file format and every response to an input reveals how it was made. An AI model can read that blueprint, and it does not need to see the source code to do so. The makers of the Adobe copies say exactly that: they worked from public documentation and the observed behaviour of the programs, not from Adobe's code.

At large organisations the same thing is happening, but with their own legacy systems. [AWS describes](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/migration-and-modernization/reimagine-mainframe-workloads-with-a-highly-automated-workflow-using-aws-transform-and-devin/) how AI recovers the business rules from decades-old business software, traceable line by line, so that new software can then be built on top of them. What was unreadable for years becomes a document the business can say yes or no to.

On the [All-In podcast of 10 October](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TBLHdXABwAg&t=4901s), investor Chamath Palihapitiya drew the sharpest conclusion. According to him, intellectual property in software had been "effectively rendered worthless", and All-In also shared that fragment [as a separate clip](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/allinpod_chamath-software-ip-just-became-worthless-activity-7514664143635918848-GHr0). That is an exaggeration. A copy that exists is not yet a customer who switches. But the direction is right, and that is the problem for every software company.

## Building becomes a specification

If anyone can rebuild what you have built, the customer no longer pays for the building. They pay for what comes before it and what comes after it.

Before: knowing what needs to be built and why. What problem the customer really has, which exceptions sit in their process, which rules were once introduced after that one incident. That is the specification, and it is scarce.

After: making sure it keeps working. Data that stays secure, integrations with other systems, someone who is accountable when things go wrong. Licenseware names exactly those things as what remains standing when code is cheap to copy.

The building itself moves to the middle and becomes an execution step of the specification. Important, but less and less distinctive.

For the leadership of a software company, that is not a technical detail. It affects the business model. Many software companies earn their money from hours: hours of analysis, hours of building, hours of testing, hours of maintenance. If building takes less and less time and the customer reads about it in the paper, it becomes hard to keep billing the same hours. The customer will ask why a change takes three weeks, when a small team put down about ten applications in roughly a week.

The honest answer is to stop selling hours and start selling outcomes. Charging for what the software delivers for the customer: a process that runs faster, fewer errors, a decision that is better grounded. That asks something different of a software company. You have to understand your customer's business better than you do now. You have to dare to say what something delivers, and be held accountable for it. And you have to organise your own company around thinking and taking responsibility, not around filling timesheets.

That is uncomfortable. An hourly model is predictable, for the supplier as well as for the buyer. But a predictable model that represents less and less value is not a safe choice. It is a postponed one.

## The question for the boardroom

Look at your own budget. How many people, how many hours and how much cost go into the building itself? How much goes into understanding the customer and carrying responsibility for the outcome?

If the first group is large and the second small, the question is not whether AI will change that, but who does it first: you or a competitor.

Can today's software company still justify its own cost structure five years from now?
